# Globe zoom detail

What the globe shows as a reader zooms in, and the rules that keep it light on phones. The numbers live in `src/lib/globe-detail.ts`; the drawing lives in `src/lib/globe.js`.

## Camera distances

Distance is measured from the middle of the globe, so the surface is at 1. The start view is 3.45 (3.7 on phones), flying to a story ends at 3.05, and the closest zoom is 1.45 (it was 1.65 before the sharper Earth). Detail starts only after a deliberate zoom, so opening the page or flying to a story never downloads it.

| Layer | Starts to appear | Fully shown | File | Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lighter Earth | always | always | `public/assets/earth.jpg` (2048 x 1024) | about 340 KB |
| Sharp Earth | fetched at 2.7 or closer | swapped in when loaded | `public/assets/earth-sharp.jpg` (4096 x 2048) | about 1.4 MB |
| Country borders | fetched at 2.95 or closer, fade from 2.9 | 2.3 (never above 60% opacity) | `public/assets/borders.json` | about 105 KB, 33 KB compressed |
| Country and city names | fetched at 2.95 or closer, first names at 2.85 | each name fades in over 0.12 of zoom | `public/assets/places.json` | about 16 KB, 8 KB compressed |

Every layer is fetched once. A failure keeps what is already showing and is not retried. The sharp Earth is skipped when the reader's phone has Data Saver on or the graphics card cannot hold a 4096 pixel texture. `data-earth-detail` (`base`, `loading`, `sharp`, `failed`), `data-borders` and `data-places` (both `idle`, `loading`, `ready`, `failed`) on the globe element show the state, which the browser checks use.

## Where the data comes from

- **Earth pictures:** NASA Blue Marble Next Generation (January, with land height and ocean depth). `earth-sharp.jpg` is the file `example/img/earth-blue-marble.jpg` from the npm package `three-globe@2.45.6`, and `earth.jpg` is a downscale of it. NASA's own download site could not be reached from the build workspace, so the copy was not compared byte for byte with NASA's file. Details and the verification step are in `public/ASSETS.md`.
- **Country borders:** Natural Earth 4.1.0 (public domain), 1:50 million scale, through the npm package `world-atlas@2.0.2` (a development tool only). `scripts/build-borders.mjs` keeps only the borders two countries share (coastlines are in the Earth picture already) and writes integers in hundredths of a degree, with each point stored as the step from the one before. Rebuild with `npm run borders:build`. `npm run borders:check` and the test suite fail if the committed file is not exactly what the script produces.

Natural Earth draws boundaries as they are on the ground today and disclaims accuracy, and some borders are disputed. The About panel says boundaries are approximate. A different disclaimer is a copy change in `src/App.tsx` and may need General Counsel.

## Country and city names

Names are small pieces of text (not part of the picture), so they stay sharp at any zoom. The numbers live in `src/lib/places.ts` and the drawing in `src/lib/globe.js`.

- **When a name may show:** every name has a tier from 1 to 4 and appears only once the camera is at or below the tier's distance. Countries: 2.85, 2.5, 2.15, 1.85. Cities: 2.45, 2.05, 1.8, 1.62 (so every tier is fully shown by the closest zoom, 1.45). The start view (3.45, or 3.7 on phones) and the flight to a story (3.05) show no names. A name fades in over 0.12 of zoom, and fades out as it nears the edge of the globe.
- **Where it shows:** on every drawn frame the globe works out which names fit. Names are tried in priority order (tier 1 first, bigger places first), and a name is kept only if it lies fully inside the globe area and does not overlap an earlier name, a story pin (including the label beside the selected pin), or any button, the view switch or the story card drawn on top of the globe. So a crowded spot always keeps the more important name, and nothing is half hidden.
- **How it is drawn:** plain text elements in a layer under the pins. The layer ignores touches and mouse clicks, so rotating, tapping a pin and swiping a story all behave as before, and screen readers skip it (the stories are in the list and on the pins). Country names are small spaced capitals, city names are plain text beside a small dot.
- **Data:** `scripts/build-places.mjs` builds `public/assets/places.json` from two Natural Earth 5.1.2 files (public domain), each checked against a SHA-256 recorded in the script. Rebuild with `npm run places:build` (it downloads the files); `npm run places:check` fails if the committed file differs. The rules for what gets a name (only places with an ISO 3166-1 country code; national capitals plus cities of 2 million or more; a short table of shortened or corrected names) are in the header of the script and in `public/ASSETS.md`, and General Counsel should confirm them.
- **Failure:** a failed or damaged names file simply shows no names and is not retried.

## The night sky

The stars behind the globe are decorative: a made-up scatter, not a map of the real sky. `src/lib/stars.ts` builds three layers from a fixed seed (so everyone sees the same sky): 8,000 faint stars, 2,000 medium ones and 360 bright ones, all on a sphere of radius 18 behind the camera (the camera never goes past 5). About 40% of the faint stars are pulled into a soft band tilted 62 degrees, like a galaxy seen edge on, and each star has a pale blue, white or warm tint. Only a small slice of the sky is in view at once (the camera sees about 40 degrees), which is why there are thousands. A fourth layer of 24 brilliant stars stands out like the brightest stars in a real sky. On top of the made-up scatter sit eleven real constellations, 76 stars in all, drawn from the published positions of the real stars (`src/lib/constellations.ts`; Hipparcos catalogue positions as distributed in d3-celestial 0.7.35, with the source, the licence and what was not checked in `public/ASSETS.md`). They are Orion, the Big Dipper (Ursa Major), Cassiopeia, the Little Dipper (Ursa Minor), the Southern Cross (Crux), Scorpius, Leo, the Northern Cross (Cygnus), Lyra, Taurus and Canis Major. Every row of the table carries the star's Hipparcos number, so each one can be looked up again. A dot is bigger the brighter the star is (five sizes, all bigger than the bright scatter stars), and its tint follows the catalogue's colour index, from blue-white through white to orange (Betelgeuse, Antares, Aldebaran and Gacrux are among the orange ones). Each figure keeps its real shape and is scaled about its own middle so that all of them look about the same size: Orion is drawn at 45% of life size, the large figures (the Big Dipper, Scorpius, Leo, Cygnus) at 35% to 38%, and the two small ones (the Southern Cross and Lyra) are enlarged. The rest of the sky is invented, so where the figures sit in it is arbitrary, but they keep their real places relative to one another: one turn carries Orion's belt to the upper left of the opening view, just outside the globe, and the same turn is applied to every figure. Orion stands upright with east on the left, as seen from the northern hemisphere; the other figures are turned with it, so they are not each upright. The camera only ever sees slices of this sky and the globe covers the middle, so in a simulation of random views a constellation star shows beside the globe in about half of them (47% of phone-shaped views and 55% of desktop-shaped ones). A star that has the globe in front of it is hidden, like any other star. Unit tests check the data (no star listed twice, positions and sizes in range, a brighter star never drawn smaller than a fainter one in the same figure), that every drawn shape matches the real shape at its scale, that no two figures touch, that the figures keep their real places relative to each other, that Orion's belt sits at its catalogued positions (it was once about a degree off, found and fixed when the other figures were added), and that all seven Orion stars stay clear of the globe at the phone start (3.7), the desktop start (3.45) and after the flight to a story (3.05). Nothing moves, so the sky costs nothing while the globe is still and needs no reduced-motion handling. No data is fetched.

## Made-up satellites

Fourteen small pale dots can drift round the Earth. They start switched off every visit; a satellite button in the globe controls (under the compass and expand buttons, `aria-pressed`, turns yellow when on) shows and hides them, and the choice is not remembered. They are made-up satellites for atmosphere: they are not real objects, have no names, use no data source and are not tied to any real orbit, and they move far faster than real satellites (a lap takes two and a half to seven minutes) so the motion is visible. `src/lib/satellites.ts` builds their orbits from a fixed seed, so everyone sees the same ones, and the dots sit between 1.07 and 1.24 from the middle of the globe, so they pass in front of and behind the Earth (the Earth hides them). They are drawn in the globe scene with no labels, are not buttons and are skipped by screen readers, like the stars. While hidden they are not moved or drawn. They update about ten times a second when shown; with reduced motion they stay still. Nothing is fetched and no credit or data terms apply. Real satellites from CelesTrak were considered and dropped for now because they would need a General Counsel terms check; they can be revisited.

## Space stories

A NASA story that has no place on Earth and whose headline names something in space (Mars, the Moon, a rover, a telescope, a galaxy, a comet and similar; the word list is in `src/lib/space-stories.ts`) used to have no pin and showed only in the list. It now also floats above the globe as a small star (a star glyph, 1.6 from the middle of the globe), its card and list entry show its topic where a place would go ("Space exploration", "Deep space" or "Sun and space weather", chosen from the headline by `spaceTopic`; never a place name). There is no star button in the globe controls: the stars are found on the globe, and in the list. Selecting a space story flies the camera back to distance 3.9 and round the globe until the star sits in the dark about 18 degrees to the left of the centre, so it is clearly in the sky and not over a country. The star's spot depends only on the story id. A star is hidden only when the Earth is in the way.

The rule is deliberately narrow so nothing is labelled wrongly: only NASA stories, only with no pin, only headline words for things that are not on Earth. Words NASA also uses for Earth stories ("satellite", "orbit", "astronaut", "space station", "rocket") are left out, so a satellite-based forest study gets no star. A person called Venus on a NASA story would match (a known limit). No data is fetched.

## Limits worth knowing

- A picture of the whole planet runs out of detail up close. The sharp Earth is about twice as crisp as the light one at the closest zoom and still soft. Street-level detail needs map tiles, which would mean phones contacting a tile service (a Content Security Policy change that goes through General Counsel first) or tiles built from NASA's 21600 x 10800 file.
- Names cover countries and the biggest cities only. Small towns, regions, rivers and oceans are not named, and a city under a story pin or a button is skipped on purpose.
- Nothing here contacts another origin: every file is served from the app, so `connect-src 'self'` and `img-src 'self'` are unchanged.
- Borders are drawn as 1 pixel lines just above the surface, split into pieces of at most 1.5 degrees so long straight borders follow the curve of the globe.
